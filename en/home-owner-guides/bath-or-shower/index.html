Bath or shower in a private house bathroom - what the litres and the tariff show

A bath or a shower in a private house is a decision about space and habits, but also about water.

A full bath uses about 80 litres, and the same flows out in eight minutes of a shower at 10 litres a minute (a rough calculation from the data of Waterwise and CCW in Great Britain). With a water-saving shower head, at about 6 litres a minute, the same shower takes 48 litres.

Litres in one wash

The British water-saving organisation Waterwise estimates that a typical bath uses about 80 litres, a standard shower head 10–15 litres a minute, and a water-saving one about 6 litres a minute. The Consumer Council for Water (CCW) notes that a powerful or mixer-tap shower can give about 15 litres a minute. So a shower saves more than a bath only if it is short or has a low flow.

In a house with its own well and pump, the shower flow is also set by the pump: at high pressure the shower’s use approaches the upper figure. A shower head’s class can be chosen by the European voluntary water label (Unified Water Label), which Topten.eu uses in its selection criteria:

Dark green
up to 6 l/min.
Light green
up to 8 l/min.
Yellow
up to 10 l/min.
Orange
up to 13 l/min.
Red
over 13 l/min.

If the measured flow is over 10 l/min, a more economical shower head will save more than replacing the bath. How to measure the flow with a bucket is in the answer below.

One bath at Ķekavas nami tariffs

In a house connected to the network, every litre is paid for twice: for water and for sewerage. The SIA “Ķekavas nami” tariff in the villages of Ķekava parish and in Daugmale from 1 January 2026 is €1.09/m³ for water and €3.04/m³ for sewerage, excluding VAT. So an 80-litre bath costs about €0.33 excluding VAT, and a 48-litre shower about €0.20.

In Baloži the tariffs are €0.59 and €1.60/m³, so the same bath costs about €0.18. For Baldone and Baldone parish the Ķekavas nami page gives €1.68 and €2.96/m³ excluding VAT (page viewed on 24 November 2026), and a bath costs about €0.37. These are rough calculations for water and sewerage only; the cost of heating the hot water is not included.

In a house with its own well and septic tank nobody sends a bill for cubic metres, but the same litres go through your pump, water heater and septic tank.

When the bath stays and when a shower comes

A bath needs hot water in large quantity at once, a shower gradually. If the house has a small electric water heater, one bath can empty it, and the next person has no hot water until the heater warms up. From the same heater, showers one after another are enough for several people if the flow is not high. Before deciding, find out the volume of the water heater and whether there is still enough hot water after one bath.

For a small child or a person who finds it hard to stand for long, a bath can be more convenient. Then a shower can be set up in the bath itself with a shower column and a glass screen, and both are in one place.

If there is one bathroom and its bath is rarely used, a shower frees up space and water. If the house has two bathrooms, you can keep both: a shower for every day, a bath in the other room.

The drain sets the rebuild and its price

The decision is also set by where the waste outlet runs: a shower tray, or a tiled shower zone with a drain in the floor, needs a waste in a different place and at a different height than a bath. So before deciding, find out where the bath’s waste outlet is and whether a tray or a floor drain can be connected to it.

Removing the bath and reworking the connections for a shower has no single price. It is set by taking out and carrying away the old bath, by whether a shower cabin, a tray or a tiled area with a floor drain goes in its place, and by whether the waste outlet has to be moved or lowered. Individual fixtures have starting prices: a shower cabin with a tray from €125, a shower column with an overhead shower from €60, a new bath with a waste connection from €60.

Questions: Bath or shower in a private house bathroom

How can I find out how many litres a minute our shower gives?

With a bucket and a clock: hold the bucket under the fully open shower for 10 seconds, measure the water with a measuring jug and multiply by six. If it comes to more than 13 litres a minute, by the water label it is the red class.

Does a running WC really waste as much water as a bath?

More. Waterwise estimates that a running WC can waste up to 400 litres a day, that is, about five baths. If water runs continuously in the WC cistern, it is worth fixing that first, before thinking about replacing a bath for the sake of water.

Is the water label for shower heads mandatory?

No. The European water label (Unified Water Label) is voluntary, not EU law. Topten.eu uses it as a basis for sorting taps and showers into classes by maximum flow.
